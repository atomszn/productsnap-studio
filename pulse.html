Frontier Tech

  • How Starlink Works — The cell tower moves to the sky.

    Follow Ila, on a farm 40 km from town, as her data leaves a dish on the roof, hops to a moving satellite, lands at a ground station and joins the internet. Then see how her phone reaches space, how Starlink differs from its rivals, who pays, where it is heading and what could stop it.

    • Starlink beams internet from thousands of satellites flying about 480 km up, so homes far from cables can get online with a small dish.
    • Each satellite passes overhead in minutes; the dish hands off between them, and the data drops to ground stations wired to fiber.
    • Ordinary phones are next: texting via satellite works today, and fuller phone service is SpaceX's goal for around 2028.
  • How SpaceX Rockets Work — Land it, fly it again, fill it up.

    Nia follows the money and the hardware for her science-fair poster: why a $74 million rocket is mostly hardware, how landing and tower catches make reuse pay, how SpaceX compares with the world's big rockets, why a Moon trip needs 6 to 22 launches to refill in orbit, and how the 2028 landing would work.

    • A Falcon 9 launch lists at $74 million; propellant (fuel plus oxygen) is under 1% and the raw materials about 2%, so the cost is mostly hardware.
    • Reuse means only the upper stage is lost. One booster has flown 37 times; Starship's Super Heavy booster is caught by its tower.
    • Starship needs 6 to 22 launches to refill in orbit for the Moon; NASA plans a 2027 docking test and a first landing in early 2028.
  • How The Boring Company Digs — Smaller tunnels, no pits, no stopping. Does it scale?

    Wren's field journal tests one mile of tunnel: why subways cost $100 million to $1 billion+ a mile, how a TBM (tunnel boring machine) digs, Boring's smaller hole and Prufrock machine, how the Vegas Loop works, what a mile costs from Madrid to New York, what Boring promised vs delivered, and the critiques.

    • Tunnels are slow and costly: a standard TBM (tunnel boring machine) digs a mile in 8 to 12 weeks, and big-city lines cost $100 million to $1 billion+ a mile, mostly in stations.
    • Boring shrinks the tunnel to 12 feet and builds its own Prufrock machines; the first Vegas Loop cost about $60 million a route mile, and Prufrock digs about a mile a month against a mile-a-week goal.
    • The Vegas Loop has 14 stations and 4 million+ riders, but cars carry far fewer people than trains (4,431 an hour audited vs up to 36,000), and safety and oversight questions remain.
  • How Neuralink Works — Hair-thin threads, a robot surgeon and a cursor moved by thought.

    Juno's lab sketchbook uses one text message as a yardstick: how the brain says "move", Neuralink's implant, robot surgeon and decoder, the real trial speeds, how it compares with the Utah array, Synchron and EEG (brain-wave) caps, where trials and safety stand, and what was promised vs delivered.

    • Paralysis cuts the path from brain to muscle, but the motor cortex still fires; a BCI (brain-computer interface) reads those spikes and turns them into actions.
    • Neuralink's N1 puts 1,000+ electrodes on hair-thin threads placed by a robot; the record is 11.32 BPS (bits per second), close to a mouse, and typing reaches 40 words a minute.
    • Neuralink had 21 people enrolled (Jan 2026) but no approval to sell; safety, animal welfare, price and Musk's timelines are still open questions.
  • How Electric Cars Work — Lithium on a shuttle, magnets that chase, and one 400-mile road trip.

    Remy's road-trip log uses one 400-mile drive to Grandma's as a yardstick: why EVs (electric vehicles) were stuck, how batteries and motors work, how Tesla's plan and chargers changed the game, what the trip costs vs gas, how the world (and China's BYD) switched, and cameras vs lidar (laser radar) for self-driving.

    • Around 2010 EVs (electric vehicles) were stuck on short range, costly batteries and slow charging; battery packs are now 93% cheaper.
    • A battery shuttles lithium ions, a motor chases a spinning magnetic field, and an EV turns 87 to 91% of its energy into motion vs about 30% for gas.
    • Tesla proved the cars and chargers; now China and BYD lead sales, and the next race is self-driving, Waymo's lidar (laser radar) vs Tesla's cameras.
  • How Humanoid Robots Work — Controlled falling, fussy hands and chores that still wait.

    Mo's workshop log tests humanoid robots against three chores: why robots get human shapes, the hard parts (balance, hands, motors and batteries), how AI (artificial intelligence) brains think and learn, the 2026 line-up from Optimus to Unitree, real work vs promises, demos, and safety, cost and hype.

    • Humanoids are shaped like us because the world is built for us, but walking, hands and battery life are still hard.
    • Robots learn in simulation, from human drivers and videos, and from big AI (artificial intelligence) models that split thinking slow from moving fast.
    • As of Oct 2026 only 13,000 to 18,000 shipped in 2025, mostly from China; real jobs are in factories and warehouses, and homes are still a promise.
  • Nuclear for AI — Chatbots, chain reactions and a very long waiting line.

    Pip's lab notes follow AI's (artificial intelligence's) electricity hunger back to nuclear power: the 24/7 firm-power problem, how a reactor works, why big plants got slow, small modular reactors' promise vs reality, the fuel and permit gates, big-tech deals, a nuclear vs gas vs solar vs wind comparison, waste and honest risks, as of Oct 2026.

    • AI (artificial intelligence) data centres need huge amounts of power around the clock, and nuclear runs about 91% of the time with no carbon emissions.
    • A reactor is a kettle: splitting atoms makes heat, steam spins a turbine, and control rods keep the chain reaction on a leash.
    • As of Oct 2026 big tech has signed gigawatts of deals, but new small reactors are still years away, so old plants, restarts and gas fill the gap.

AI

  • AI Basics — Maths, not magic.

    Follow Leo as he asks a chatbot what to cook for 4 friends, and see how AI learns, how it answers, who is behind it and what the rules are around the world.

    • A chatbot runs on an LLM (large language model) that learned by guessing missing words.
    • It answers one word at a time, so it can sound sure and be wrong.
    • Give it details, keep secrets out, and check what matters.
  • When AI Gets It Wrong — Confident isn't correct.

    Follow Mia as a chatbot helps with her honeybee report, invents a book, agrees too easily and tips a story one way, and learn how to check it like a detective.

    • Chatbots can make up answers that sound true (hallucinations).
    • They can agree too easily and repeat old biases from their training data.
    • Open the source, ask how sure it is, and check a second place.
  • Using AI Yourself — The higher you climb, the more you check.

    Tomas climbs from one homework question to a weekly study routine. Learn the ladders and the snakes of using a chatbot yourself.

    • Chatbots can sound sure and still be wrong.
    • Let AI quiz you, not think for you.
    • Know what happens to your chats, and what to keep private.
  • Searching with AI — The summary is a starting card, not the book.

    Elif asks if she can pack a power bank for a flight. See how AI search answers are built, where the ads go, and why the source still matters.

    • AI answers are built from many searches and linked pages.
    • With an AI summary, people clicked fewer links.
    • Open the cited page and check the official source.
  • AI, Jobs & School — Use AI to think harder, not to skip the thinking.

    Ines writes a final essay and applies for a weekend office job. See how AI is changing schoolwork, first jobs and the rules around both.

    • Schools set their own AI rules; check yours.
    • AI tends to change the tasks in a job more than erase it.
    • Employers still want people who can explain their thinking.
  • Deepfakes & Scams — Checking beats spotting.

    Ravi gets a call that sounds just like his aunt. Spot the clues, see the scammer's playbook, and set up a simple family plan.

    • AI can copy a voice or face from a short clip.
    • Rush, secrecy and odd payment methods are the biggest clues.
    • Hang up, call back on a known number, and use a family safe word.
  • Your Data, Privacy & Copyright — Your data, your art, your face.

    Walk through Noor's first art show: what happens to what she types, whether her foxes trained an AI, who owns AI-made art, and the light on a visitor's smart glasses.

    • Chats can train AI unless you switch it off.
    • Prompts alone don't make you an author; human work does.
    • Smart-glasses lights help, but asking is better.
  • AI and Your Health — Prepare with AI. Decide with a clinician.

    Kofi asks a chatbot about a cough that won't go away. See what it can and can't do, how nurses and regulators use and check AI, and when to skip the chatbot entirely.

    • Chatbots can explain, but they can't examine you.
    • In one study, people with chatbots decided no better than people without.
    • In an emergency, call your local emergency number.
  • AI Agents — A chatbot answers. An agent does.

    Follow Zara, a student, as an AI agent books dinner for 8 on Saturday, seen from her side, the AI company's, the restaurant's, the bank's and the referees' around the world.

    • An AI agent does the job (books the table), not just chats.
    • You pay by subscription, or ads pay for free plans, so ask whose side it is on.
    • When it slips up, the restaurant is the first stop, then the bank; keep the agent on a valet key.
  • How an AI Agent Is Built — A good agent is a short recipe with a human taster.

    Omar builds an AI helper for his family's bike shop that handles one return, start to finish. See the ingredients, the rules and where a person stays in charge.

    • An agent is a model with tools and instructions.
    • Give it only the access the job needs.
    • For money moves, a person should approve first.
  • When AI Does the Shopping — Always confirm the buy; keep the receipt.

    Jonah lets an AI watch the price of rain boots and buy them for him. Then he gets charged twice. Who sells, who pays, and how you get your money back.

    • A shopping agent can watch prices and buy only after you confirm.
    • The AI shops; the store still sells.
    • Keep your receipts and know your dispute window.
  • What Powers AI — One question is small; billions add up.

    Hana asks an AI one question for her science fair. Follow it to a data centre and see the chips, electricity and water behind every answer.

    • One AI question travels to a data centre where chips, electricity and cooling answer it.
    • One prompt is small; billions add up.
    • Look for figures with a date and a source.
  • Robots & Physical AI — Real robots are often boring arms.

    Kai orders a water bottle and a robot arm called Rivet picks it. See how robots learn, what the makers say, and who keeps workers safe.

    • Physical AI moves real things; a world model predicts what happens next.
    • Ask if a robot is a demo, a pilot or a product.
    • Workers and robots often share the job.
  • Open vs Closed AI — Read the licence; know where your data goes.

    Rosa wants AI to help answer her bakery's emails. Should she download an open model or pay for a closed one? Compare cost, privacy, control and the rules.

    • Open-weight models can be downloaded and run yourself; closed ones are used via an app or API.
    • Read the licence before you build on a model.
    • Know where your data goes.
  • How AI Compute Works — Stoves, gas lines, counter space and a kitchen in orbit.

    Chef Kai's kitchen explains AI (artificial intelligence) compute: chips as stoves (Nvidia's Hopper, Blackwell and Rubin), electricity as the gas line, high bandwidth memory (HBM) as counter space, cooling the building, scarce land, and orbital data centers as the long shot, as of Nov 2026.

    • Compute is the math chips do to cook each AI (artificial intelligence) answer: GPUs (graphics processing units) are the stoves, and Nvidia makes most of them.
    • Each new Nvidia chip burns more power but, Nvidia says, uses less per answer; electricity, memory, cooling and land are now the tight spots.
    • As of Nov 2026, builders chase power with nuclear deals, their own gas plants and spare grid room, while orbit is still a test kitchen.

Fintech

  • Tokenization — Same fund, new receipt.

    Amara has $1,000 saved and spots a tokenized fund in her app. Is it a new kind of money, or the same fund with a new receipt? Here's what changes and what doesn't.

    • Tokenizing a fund changes how ownership is recorded, not what the fund holds.
    • Big firms like BlackRock, Franklin Templeton and DTCC are trying it, mostly for large investors so far.
    • A tokenized security is still a security: ask who keeps the record and who can fix mistakes.
  • Fintech Rails — Five roads for one $600 bike.

    Felix buys a $600 bike from a small shop. The same money can travel five different roads: card, ACH, wire, instant transfer or stablecoin. Here's how fast each one is, what it costs, and which one has an undo.

    • A card tap is approved in seconds, but the money follows later, in batches between banks.
    • Instant rails like RTP and FedNow move money in seconds, any day, but those payments are final.
    • Speed and safety pull in opposite directions: card buyers can dispute charges, instant payers usually can't.
  • Stablecoins — A $1 ticket that never sleeps.

    Follow Ana's $100 as it becomes a digital dollar: what backs it, who earns the interest, the rules around the world, how an AI agent can spend it, and what it means for you.

    • A stablecoin is a digital claim ticket worth $1, backed mostly by short US government IOUs and cash.
    • The interest on that backing goes to the issuer and its partners; holders get none, by law in the US and EU.
    • It is fast and works 24/7, but it is not a bank account: no deposit insurance, and it can wobble below $1.

Macro

  • Why Your Mortgage Moves — Your rate rides the bond market, not just the Fed.

    Diego is buying his first home. Why did his mortgage rate jump, when the Fed was cutting? Follow his rate from the bank to the bond market.

    • A mortgage rate is set by investors in the bond market, not directly by the Fed.
    • Mortgage rates tend to move with the 10-year Treasury yield.
    • Get at least three written offers and compare the APR.
  • The Debt Loop — A second card paying the first. What could go wrong?

    Why borrowing to pay interest feeds itself, told through one student's $20 a week.

    • Borrowing to pay interest makes debt grow on itself: in 2026 about half of new US borrowing just covered interest.
    • When lots of borrowed money moves at once, a calm market can suddenly panic.
    • New money reaches some people before prices rise, and others after.
  • Dollar, Gold and Yen — Two rulers, one canary, and a trip to Japan.

    Lucia is saving dollars for a trip to Japan. Why does her money buy more yen this year, why do central banks keep buying gold, and why do people watch the yen?

    • A dollar can look strong against other money and still buy less at the shop.
    • Money tends to move toward higher interest rates, which moves exchange rates.
    • A sudden jump in the yen can be an early sign of market stress.
  • Two Schools of Economics — One closed factory, two readings, and a shared rooftop.

    A factory closes and Grace's café goes quiet. Two schools of economics read the same slump in different ways. Hear both, then decide what you think.

    • Keynesians focus on the slump: when spending falls, boost it, and ease off when things run hot.
    • Austrians look at the boom before: cheap credit can lead firms to build more than people want.
    • Both want steady prices and jobs; they differ on how, and real countries mix both.
  • Why is everything so expensive? — Same cart. Bigger receipt. Bruh.

    Why prices keep climbing, told through Sam's $100 Saturday shop.

    • Inflation means the same money buys less: US prices roughly doubled since 2000.
    • The 2021-22 spike had several causes stacked together: money and demand, supply chains, energy and some company pricing.
    • Inflation quietly moves buying power between shoppers, savers, workers, borrowers and governments.
